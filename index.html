How ReMind works

ReMind looks for patterns in mood and behavior that research has linked to Alzheimer's disease. Here's how it was built, in plain language.

1

It learned from real patients

ReMind studied the records of 37,159 people who visited Alzheimer's research centers across the U.S. Some were later diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease and some were not. ReMind learned which answers were more common in each group.

2

It asks simple questions

All 22 questions can be answered at home. There are no blood tests, brain scans, or genetic tests. Here's what ReMind looks at:

AgeSexYears of educationAnxietyLow moodApathyAgitationRepetitive movementsNight-time disruptionsAppetite changesDepression scale (GDS)Feeling full of energyFear something bad will happen
3

It was tested on new people

To make sure it really works, ReMind was tested five separate times, each time on a group of people it had never seen before.

4

Your answers stay private

ReMind runs inside this web page. Your answers are scored on your own device and are never sent anywhere.

How accurate is it?

Picture 100 pairs of people. In each pair, one person later developed Alzheimer's disease and one did not. ReMind gives the higher score to the right person in about 82 of the 100 pairs.

This is called the AUC. ReMind's AUC is 0.82.

How to read your result

  • Your score goes from 0 to 100. A higher score means your answers look more like those of people who later developed Alzheimer's disease.
  • Scores of 50 or more are shown as a higher risk pattern.
  • The "What influenced this result" bars show which of your answers raised or lowered your score.

What ReMind can't do

  • It can't diagnose Alzheimer's disease. Only a doctor can.
  • It learned from people who volunteered at research centers, who may not be like everyone.
  • It doesn't use memory tests, brain scans, or genetics, which doctors use for a diagnosis.
  • Answers can change with how someone feels that day.
  • The Spanish and Chinese versions are translations. ReMind learned mostly from data collected in English.

For researchers: technical details
  • Data: National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) Uniform Data Set, first visits. Outcome: eventual Alzheimer's disease diagnosis vs. cognitively normal.
  • Inputs (13): age, sex, and years of education; NPI-Q severity (0–3) of anxiety, depression, apathy, and agitation; NPI-Q presence (0/1) of motor disturbance, nighttime behaviors, and appetite changes; GDS-15 total (0–15) plus the GDS energy and fear items.
  • Preprocessing: NPI-Q severity recoded to 0 when the symptom was absent; remaining missing codes excluded; standardization fit within each training fold.
  • Models: logistic regression (binomial GLM) and a multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers (64 and 32 units, batch normalization, ReLU, dropout 0.3 and 0.2), trained with AdamW and binary cross-entropy, with early stopping on validation loss.
  • Validation: stratified 5-fold cross-validation. Mean AUC: logistic regression 0.81, MLP 0.82. A research model that also used APOE ε4 count reached 0.84; ReMind leaves it out because users can't know their genotype without testing.
  • Deployment: batch normalization folded into the linear layers; weights exported to JSON and run in JavaScript, checked against PyTorch outputs.
  • Explanations: each input is replaced with a reference value (a healthy 65-year-old) and the change in log-odds is shown.
